What Is Probate?

California Probate: What Families Need to Know

Probate is the court-supervised process of settling a deceased person's estate — paying debts and distributing assets to heirs. In California, probate is required when a person dies with assets in their name alone (not held in a trust or with a beneficiary designation) totaling more than $184,500. It is a public process, and it takes time.

California probate takes 12 to 24 months on average and costs 4 to 8% of the gross estate value in statutory attorney and executor fees — even if the estate is simple. A living trust completely avoids this process.

How to Avoid Probate in California

  • Create a revocable living trust and transfer assets into it
  • Add beneficiary designations to retirement accounts and life insurance
  • Use joint tenancy or community property with right of survivorship for real property
  • Establish payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts for bank and brokerage accounts
  • For small estates (under $184,500), a simplified affidavit procedure may be available

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions from families facing probate in Encino and greater Los Angeles.

Simple estates can take 9 to 12 months. Complex estates, contested matters, or those involving real property often take 18 to 24 months or longer. Court scheduling and creditor notification periods are fixed by law and cannot be shortened.

California law sets statutory fees for both the attorney and the executor based on the gross value of the estate: 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, and 1% of the next $9 million. On a $1 million estate, that is roughly $46,000 in combined fees — before extraordinary fees for complex matters.

Probate is a court-supervised process for estates without a trust. Trust administration is a private process conducted by the trustee — no court involvement required. Trust administration is faster, less expensive, and entirely private.

If someone dies "intestate" (without a will), California's intestacy laws determine how assets are distributed — typically to the spouse and children according to a statutory formula. The probate court will appoint an administrator. The result may not reflect what the deceased would have wanted.
